\begin{block}{Introduction} \label{intro}
 88
Ice cover in the Great Lakes has significant influence on regional climate, ecology, and economy and has been recorded with high accuracy since the advent of remote-sensing data in the early 1970's. This data is spatially resolved at 1.25KM resolution and available as daily files throughout each ice season\supercite{Yang2020}.
 89
 90
Previous work has assessed large-scale (i.e. lake or basin-wide) ice cover fluctuations through time and their relationship to global weather phenomena \supercite{Sharma2019, OReilly2015, Wang2018, Bai2012}. Mason et al. (2016)\supercite{Mason2016} used the Great Lakes Aquatic Habitat Framework (GLAHF)\supercite{Wang2015} to analyze ice cover duration for 1973-2013. The delineation of subregions in GLAHF includes multiple spatial scales and was designed by considering physical, ecological and socioeconomic factors.
 91
 92
Since 2013, the Great Lakes have seen two extreme highs (2014 and 2015) and two extreme lows (2024 and 2017) in the top 5 highest/lowest for seasonal max ice (see fig. \ref{fig:amic}). This work provides an update to Mason et al. 2016 for recent years, analysis of two additional ice metrics (seasonal max and mean), and correlations to several meteorological variables from 530 met stations.

\justifying

%\vspace{5ex}
126
To address the first research question, the below metrics are calculated for each GLAHF subregion and ice season and then Mann-Kendall monotonic trend test\supercite{Mann1945} is applied to check for statistical significance:
127
\begin{itemize}
128
\item \textbf{Annual Max Ice Cover (AMIC):} maximum percent (single daily value) during a given season
129
\item \textbf{Seasonal Average Ice Cover (JFM):} mean daily percent of ice cover for Jan 1 - Mar 31
130
\item \textbf{Ice Duration:} Total (non-consecutive) days$^{\dagger}$ where ice cover exceeded 10\%
131
\end{itemize}
132
 
133
 
134
\vspace{1ex}
135
 
136
To address the second research question, station data\supercite{Menne2012,Smith2011} is aggregated for each subregion based on a 20 KM radius and correlations between ice metrics and daily mean meteorological surface variables are identified. On average, 30 stations per subregion were located within the radius but station data availability varied through time, location and variable. Temperature min/max were reported most frequently (median station count $\approx$10 per subregion). Other variables\textemdash cloud cover, precipitation, wind speed, were reported less frequently (median $\approx$5).

\footnotesize{ $\dagger$: days are only counted Jan 1 - Mar 31 due to shorter ice reporting windows during 1970's and 80's. This method likely \emph{underestimates} trends in decreasing ice duration but is required to avoid artificial identification of season length decrease.}

Season duration decrease is visible in some regions but not apparent in others (see fig. \ref{fig:dur}). Regions that show decrease are often offshore and deep: Superior (WSU, CSU, ESU), Huron (NHU, EHU, CHU), and Ontario (WON, CON). This may be due to warmer subsurface temps\supercite{Cannon2024, Anderson2021} during later years providing heat to surface waters and preventing freezing, whereas shallow regions may be able to consistently release all heat from the water column. (Despite the lack of obvious decrease, a majority of regions do show significant decreasing trends in fig. \ref{fig:trends}).
